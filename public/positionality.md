# Positionality Statement

As the instructor who wrote this focus-group protocol, taught the class whose students produced the data, and now analyzes the results, it's important to note how each of those roles shapes what gets seen and what doesn't.

The most obvious asymmetry is that the students in the room know I will read the transcript and I grade them. That almost certainly muted certain kinds of candor, i.e. how heavily they lean on AI, which course policies they quietly ignore, how they really feel about assignments and so forth. Also, I chose which questions to ask. The deductive codebook the analysis leans on is keyed to those questions, and the analysis can only find what the protocol invited. An inductive pass might surface categories this instrument cannot. I did a small inductive demo on one section to illustrate this.

**What I did not do and should have, if this were a paper rather than a teaching artifact:**

- No second human coder on any pass. Every claim resting on "I coded this way and Claude coded that way" has one human in the loop (me).
- No inter-rater reliability computed (κ, percent agreement, etc.). The disagreements between me and Claude that the site shows are just a provocation for students to look at rather than a validity check.
- The inductive pass is scoped to roughly 20 minutes of my time. A real inductive analysis would be weeks.

**What the site does to compensate (partially):**

- Publishes the raw disagreements rather than a reconciled "final" read.
- Labels the inductive section as a demo and names what rigor would require.
- Invites readers (other students, colleagues, faculty) to react to every section as a lightweight form of member checking (bringing your interpretations back to the people who produced the data and asking whether the analysis rings true to their experience).

**Takeaways**

The pedagogical goal of this exercise is to show students what qualitative coding looks like while simultaneously reporting out findings from the focus groups.
